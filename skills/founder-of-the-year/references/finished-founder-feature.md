# The article

The article is the deliverable. Everything else supports it. Write it so that a stranger who knows nothing about the founder finishes it pleased to have met them, and so that a sceptical editor who then reads the verification sheet finds nothing the article hid.

## Register

Award-profile: assured, warm, specific, celebratory where the facts earn it. The tone of a good magazine profile of someone the writer respects, not of an audit, a press release or a CV. Vary sentence length. Use concrete nouns (a booking form, a members' login, a countdown on a launch page) over abstractions (digital transformation, solutions). Let the strongest true achievements carry their proper weight and explain their stakes: what was hard, who needed it, what the founder took responsibility for, what exists now.

Bring in the founder's approved personal motivation where it explains a choice, and credit collaborators as part of the founder's success rather than as a hedge against it.

## The attribution rule

Attribute to the founder only what the interview record or their own written words contain. Then be sparing with the attribution itself:

- **State plainly** what public records, live pages, repositories or the founder's own documents establish, and what is uncontroversial biography. The verification sheet carries the source.
- **Attribute once**, in natural language, a consequential claim that rests on the founder's account alone (a delivery story, an internal capability, a client's result they describe), then move on. "By his own account" once per story, not per sentence.
- **Leave out** a claim too weak to state and too important to attribute away. Dropping a weak statistic beats hedging it.

A paragraph with more than one "he says" needs rewriting, not another caveat. The writer's own synthesis (a lesson, a summary line, a judgement about significance) reads as the writer's; never hand the founder a sentence they did not say.

## Structure

Give the piece real architecture. A long run of grey paragraphs reads cautious even when the words are confident.

1. **Label line.** "Founder-prepared feature, drafted with AI assistance." Once, small.
2. **Kicker and headline.** A truthful, intriguing promise. Not the skill's name.
3. **Standfirst.** Adds subject, work and stakes; does not repeat the headline.
4. **At a glance.** Name, where based, the businesses in a line each, and three "in brief" points.
5. **Opening.** A real, supported moment, problem or decision. No invented scene.
6. **Body sections** with short crossheads, built on cause, decision and consequence. Introduce each person and business before relying on the name. One idea per section; no mission statement repeated three times.
7. **Case-study panels.** Two or three. Each: a label (what kind of client), a title, the need, what was built, what a visitor can see today, named only with recorded permission.
8. **Timeline.** Six to ten dated milestones from registers, repositories and the founder's own records.
9. **Pull-quotes.** Only words that exist verbatim in a source (a typed message, a written motto, a transcript). None reconstructed. One or two at most.
10. **Closing.** A grounded next test or forward look, earned by the story. Not a triumph the evidence does not support, and not a disclaimer.
11. **Footer.** A one-line pointer to the verification sheet.

Typical length: 1,100 to 1,600 words of body plus the panels. Shorter if the material is thin; never padded.

## What the verification sheet takes off the article's shoulders

Move to `public/verification-sheet.md`, not the body: which claims are public record, company-published, the founder's account or a design; what was not independently checked; which figures were deliberately left out and why (without repeating them); naming permissions still open; what further reporting an editor would need. The article may say once, near the end, that a verification sheet accompanies it.

## Never

- An award, ranking, nomination, byline, publication or affiliation that was not verified.
- A figure from the founder's own sites unless the page substantiates it.
- A client, partner or family member named without recorded permission; a combination of details that identifies someone who should stay anonymous.
- A quotation that is not exact, or joined from two places.
- Private figures: revenue, fees, equity, funding, salaries, client prices.
- A future stage described as a present one (planned, built, running, launched and measured are different words).

## Review the finished article, not the plan

- **Hook:** does the headline make a promise the body keeps?
- **Significance:** are the strongest supported achievements visible, with their stakes explained?
- **Narrative:** do opening, decisions, examples, personal story and ending reinforce one theme?
- **Voice:** assured and vivid, varied rhythm, concrete language; no slogans, no stacked caveats; count the "he says" and cut the ones not doing work.
- **Protection:** no false status, invented quote, inflated metric, concealed stage, unfair credit or private leak.
- **Finish:** a memorable closing earned by the story; the HTML and PDF match the Markdown; the pack agrees with the article.

Fix findings in the article itself before calling the pass complete.
